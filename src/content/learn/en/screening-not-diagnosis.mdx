A questionnaire score is not a diagnosis. That sentence sounds obvious, and yet a number like "PHQ-9: 14" arrives looking so much like a lab result — bounded, scored, colour-coded — that it is easy to read it as a verdict. It isn't one. The validated mental-health questionnaires were built to _screen_: to flag, cheaply and repeatably, whether a closer conversation is warranted. Used that way — repeated over time, then handed to a professional — they are among the most useful things you can track. Read as a self-delivered diagnosis, they mislead in both directions.

## What these questionnaires actually measure

![](/learn-img-2/screening-not-diagnosis.jpg)

A screening instrument is a short, structured, scored self-report. It asks a fixed set of questions, sums the answers, and places the total in a severity band. The point of the structure is repeatability: the same questions, scored the same way, every time — so a score in March can be compared honestly with a score in June.[^phq9]

Four instruments cover most of the ground. The **PHQ-9** measures depressive symptoms over the past two weeks, mapped onto the nine clinical criteria for major depression.[^phq9] The **GAD-7** does the same for generalised anxiety.[^gad7] The **WHO-5** is a short positive-wellbeing index rather than a symptom count — it asks how good the last two weeks felt, not how bad.[^who5] The **PSS-10** measures _perceived_ stress, deliberately as a continuous construct with no clinical cut-off at all.[^pss] They overlap but are not interchangeable: a low WHO-5 and a high PHQ-9 are describing the same fortnight from opposite ends.

## Reading the severity bands

The published thresholds are worth knowing — and worth treating as orientation, never as a scoreboard or a label you assign yourself.

| Instrument | Range  | Action threshold                                                           | What it screens for |
| ---------- | ------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| PHQ-9      | 0–27   | ≥10 (≈88% sensitivity and specificity for major depression)[^phq9]         | Depressive symptoms |
| GAD-7      | 0–21   | ≥10 (≈89% sensitivity, ≈82% specificity)[^gad7]                            | Generalised anxiety |
| WHO-5      | 0–100% | ≤50% (raw ≤12) indicates poor wellbeing, warrants further screening[^who5] | Positive wellbeing  |
| PSS-10     | 0–40   | No clinical cut-off by design — read for trend[^pss]                       | Perceived stress    |

A score crossing its threshold means _screen positive_, not _diagnosed_. The standard PHQ-9 and GAD-7 bands beneath the cut-off (minimal, mild, moderate, moderately severe, severe) describe symptom load, not disease severity, and they were validated to start a conversation — not end one.[^phq9][^gad7]

## Why repeating it beats a single sitting

A self-report taken once is a snapshot, and snapshots are noisy. How you slept, what happened that morning, even how the question is framed all nudge the number.[^pss] The instruments were designed with this in mind: the WHO-5 treats a fall of 10 percentage points as a clinically meaningful change — that is, it is built to be read as _movement against your own earlier scores_.[^who5] The same logic carries the others. A single PHQ-9 of 11 says far less than three monthly PHQ-9s tracing a line from 6 to 11 to 16, or the reverse. Repeat administration is what turns a screen into a trend you and a clinician can act on — and it is the standard way these tools monitor whether a treatment is working.[^phq9]

This is the same trend-over-snapshot discipline that makes a [mood log](/learn/tracking-mood), a [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) baseline or any [personal trend](/learn/reading-your-trends) worth keeping: one reading is an opinion, a series is data.

## What a positive screen means — and doesn't

Screening tests trade certainty for reach. At a PHQ-9 cut-off of 10, roughly one in eight people who screen positive will not, on fuller assessment, have major depression — that is what "about 88% specificity" means in practice.[^phq9] False positives are a feature of any screen, not a flaw, which is exactly why a positive result is a _prompt to talk to a professional_, not a diagnosis you render at the kitchen table. A clinician adds what a questionnaire cannot: context, history, physical causes, and the difference between a hard fortnight and an illness.[^uspstf]

<Aside type="note">
  A high score after a bereavement, a brutal work stretch, a new baby or a
  physical illness can reflect the circumstances rather than a clinical disorder
  — and a low score does not prove all is well, especially if you answered
  quickly or hopefully. The number is a starting point for a conversation, not
  the conclusion of one. Log the context alongside the score so the trend stays
  honest.
</Aside>

## The one item that is not about trends

Most of a screening score is read as a slow-moving line. One item is not. The PHQ-9's ninth question asks directly about thoughts of being better off dead, or of self-harm.[^phq9] That item is read on its own, immediately, regardless of the total — a low overall score never cancels it out.

<Aside type="caution">
  A screening questionnaire is a way to notice things sooner, not a diagnostic
  tool, and it can never tell you what is causing a score. If low mood,
  hopelessness or loss of interest persists for two weeks or more, or starts
  getting in the way of everyday life, that is a reason to talk to a doctor or a
  mental-health professional — you do not need to wait until things feel severe,
  and reaching out is not an overreaction. And any thought of harming yourself
  or that you would be better off dead — even occasional, and whatever an
  overall score might say — is on its own a reason to reach out right now:
  contact your local emergency number or a crisis service immediately. These
  feelings are treatable, and help is available. This page cannot replace urgent
  support.
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

The instinct to read "14 out of 27" as a diagnosis is understandable — the format borrows the authority of a blood test. But the value of these questionnaires is precisely that they are _screens_: fast, repeatable, structured prompts that catch a change early and give it a shared vocabulary you can carry into a consulting room. Kept beside your [sleep](/learn/sleep-consistency), your [mood](/learn/tracking-mood) and your [stress and recovery](/learn/stress-and-recovery), a repeated screen is often the first place a downward trend becomes legible — and the easiest place to catch it while it is still easy to address.[^who]

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. These questionnaires are repeatable screening instruments, not diagnoses — a positive or rising score should prompt professional evaluation, never a self-diagnosis.
