Most vital signs announce themselves. Your heart rate climbs when you take the stairs; your blood pressure gets a cuff and a number at every check-up. Breathing rate is the quiet one. You almost never think about it — which is precisely why a shift in it carries so much information. When something is brewing, your breathing rate is often the first thing to move, and because you weren't watching, the change is honest.

## What respiratory rate actually measures

![](/learn-img-2/respiratory-rate.jpg)

Respiratory rate is the number of complete breaths you take per minute at rest. In a clinic it's counted by eye over 30 to 60 seconds; a wrist wearable estimates it overnight from the rise and fall of your chest, subtle modulation of the optical pulse signal, or the coupling between your heart and your breath.[^statpearls] It reflects how hard your body is working to keep its blood gases in balance — to take in enough oxygen and clear enough carbon dioxide. When that balance is easy, you breathe slowly and barely notice. When it's strained — by a fever, an infection, exertion, or stress — the rate quietly climbs.

It is also one of the most sensitive early signs of illness, and, historically, one of the most poorly recorded. A breathing rate that drifts up is frequently the first measurable hint that something is wrong, often before you feel unwell enough to name it.

## What's normal — and why "normal" is only half the story

The standard resting adult range is **12 to 20 breaths per minute**.[^ala] [^statpearls] But that band is wide, and where the number really earns its keep is at the edges and, more importantly, relative to your own usual figure.

| Where you sit | Breaths per minute |
| --- | --- |
| Normal resting adult | 12–20 |
| Faster than expected (tachypnoea) | over 20 |
| A common cause for concern | 25 or more |
| Slower than expected (bradypnoea) | under 12 |

Children breathe faster than adults — newborns can run 30 to 60 breaths per minute, settling toward the adult band through the teenage years. Trained endurance athletes tend to sit at the low end. Clinicians use escalation thresholds to decide when a rate demands action: a widely used sepsis screen flags a rate of 22 or more, and a standard hospital early-warning score treats both extremes — 8 or below and 25 or above — as its highest-risk readings.[^sepsis3] [^news2] Those are clinical tripwires, not everyday targets. For tracking at home, the single number matters far less than the direction it's heading.

## Why the overnight baseline is so stable

The reason respiratory rate is such a good signal is that, measured properly, it barely moves. Across healthy nights, your overnight breathing rate settles into a remarkably steady figure — your body at rest, doing the same quiet work in the same way, night after night. That stability is the whole point: a steady line makes a genuine departure easy to see.

This only works overnight. Awake, respiratory rate is one of the most state-dependent numbers your body produces — talking, moving, emotion, recent activity, even the mere awareness of being measured all shift it. A daytime spot reading is almost meaningless. Asleep, those influences fall away, which is why an overnight estimate from your device is more useful than anything you could count sitting still at your desk. That estimate is an average across the night rather than a precise clinical count, and it degrades with motion or an irregular heartbeat — but as a night-to-night baseline, it's genuinely informative.

## Read the rise, not the rate

Because resting rates vary so much between people, the most powerful way to read yours is against your own baseline rather than against the population band. A sustained rise of roughly **+3 breaths per minute above your own nightly figure** can flag that something is developing, and it often beats any absolute threshold.[^natarajan] A rate that looks perfectly normal for the population can still be a real, meaningful rise for you.

The signal is also infection-generic — it doesn't tell you *what*, only that your body is working harder than usual. In one study of people who went on to fall ill, about a third had at least one night with their respiratory rate three or more breaths above baseline within a week of symptom onset.[^miller] That is the early-warning quality in miniature: a steady line, a clear bump, a few days' head start.

Plenty of ordinary things nudge it too — a hard workout late in the day, alcohol the night before, a stuffy hot room, anxiety. As with any baseline metric, context beats precision. A trend watched under the same conditions is worth more than a single "accurate" reading taken at random.

## When a change is worth a conversation

Most overnight wobble is noise. A few patterns are worth taking seriously, and a couple are genuine emergencies.

<Aside type="caution">
A breathing rate **above 25 per minute alongside breathlessness, confusion, or a low [blood oxygen](/learn/blood-oxygen-spo2) reading** needs prompt medical attention — as does any rate at the extreme ends of clinical scoring (8 or below, 25 or above).[^news2] A fast rate on its own is sensitive but non-specific, so weigh it against how you actually feel and your other signs. **Slow, shallow breathing (bradypnoea) is the more dangerous direction** — especially the respiratory depression caused by opioids or sedatives. If someone is breathing very slowly, is hard to wake, or has bluish lips, treat it as an emergency and call for help.
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

Respiratory rate rarely moves alone. A sustained overnight rise tends to travel with a climbing [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate), a falling [heart rate variability](/learn/heart-rate-variability), and a small lift in body [temperature](/learn/body-temperature-baseline) — and when two or three of those shift together, confidence in the signal rises far more than any one of them could manage alone.[^statpearls] Read alongside a falling [blood oxygen](/learn/blood-oxygen-spo2) level, it points more specifically toward the lungs. On its own, it's the quietest number your body keeps. Watched over time, against your own steady baseline, it's one of the earliest you have.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Anchor on your own baseline; use the population ranges as a sanity check, not a scoreboard.
