Blood pressure is two numbers separated by a slash, and almost everyone can recite their own — yet almost no one is sure what the numbers mean, which one matters more, or whether the reading on the cuff this morning was even real. It is the single best-evidenced modifiable risk factor for stroke, heart attack, kidney disease and vascular dementia,[^lewington] which makes it worth understanding properly. The good news is that the underlying idea is simple. The nuance lives almost entirely in how the number is measured.

## What the two numbers actually measure

![](/learn-img-2/reading-your-blood-pressure.jpg)

Every heartbeat is two phases of one pump. The **systolic** number — the larger one on top — is the pressure in your arteries at the instant the heart contracts and pushes blood out. The **diastolic** number — the smaller one underneath — is the residual pressure while the heart relaxes and refills between beats. Both are measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg), a unit inherited from the original mercury column manometers.

So a reading of 120/80 means a peak of 120 as the heart squeezes and a trough of 80 as it rests. Neither number is "the important one" — they describe different things, and both carry risk independently. In younger adults the diastolic number often does more of the talking; from middle age onward, as arteries stiffen, the systolic number tends to climb and dominate.

Crucially, blood pressure is reported as a *category*, not as a single reading. One measurement is far too noisy to act on. A meaningful value comes from a properly taken office reading or, better, an average of validated readings at home over several days.[^esc2024]

## The guideline bands — and why two frameworks disagree

Here is where it gets genuinely confusing, because the two major guideline bodies draw the lines in different places. Both agree that hypertension as a disease begins at **140/90 mmHg** in the office. They disagree on what to call everything below it.

| Category | Range (mmHg) | Framework |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Non-elevated | under 120 and under 70 | ESC, 2024 |
| Elevated | 120–139 or 70–89 | ESC, 2024 |
| Hypertension | 140 or above, or 90 or above | ESC, 2024 |
| Normal | under 120 and under 80 | ACC/AHA, 2017 |
| Elevated | 120–129 and under 80 | ACC/AHA, 2017 |
| Stage 1 hypertension | 130–139 or 80–89 | ACC/AHA, 2017 |
| Stage 2 hypertension | 140 or above, or 90 or above | ACC/AHA, 2017 |

The US ACC/AHA framework labels 130–139/80–89 "Stage 1 hypertension"; the European ESC calls those identical numbers merely "Elevated."[^accaha] [^esc2024] The disagreement is one of *labelling and treatment intensity*, not of the underlying biology — risk rises continuously from roughly 115/75 upward, with no clean line below which it vanishes.[^lewington] HealthLog follows the European framing by default, while keeping the stricter US threshold visible, because pretending one guideline is gospel would misrepresent a field that genuinely holds two views.

One number worth knowing: the **pulse pressure**, simply systolic minus diastolic, is about 40 mmHg at rest in a 120/80 reading. A persistently wide gap (roughly 60 mmHg or more) in middle-aged and older adults is an independent marker of stiffening large arteries — a "mention it to a clinician" signal rather than a diagnosis.[^statpearls]

## Why technique matters more than physiology

This is the part most people skip, and it is the part that matters most. The way a reading is taken moves the number more than almost anything happening inside your body that day. A sloppy measurement can manufacture a hypertension diagnosis that isn't there, or hide a real one.

The conditions for a trustworthy reading are unglamorous but specific:[^esc2024]

- **Rest first.** Sit quietly for five minutes before measuring. A reading taken straight after climbing the stairs is meaningless.
- **Sit properly.** Back supported, feet flat on the floor, legs uncrossed. An unsupported back or dangling feet can each add several mmHg.
- **Arm at heart level.** A cuff held too low reads high; too high reads low. Rest the arm on a table.
- **Correct cuff size.** A cuff too small for the arm overestimates pressure substantially — this is one of the most common errors at home.
- **No talking, caffeine or smoking beforehand.** Conversation during a reading can lift systolic pressure noticeably.
- **Prefer an upper-arm cuff.** Validated upper-arm oscillometric monitors outperform wrist and optical devices, which are more sensitive to position and movement.

Then there is the **white-coat effect**: blood pressure that runs high in a clinic but is normal at home, driven by the simple stress of the setting. Its mirror image, *masked hypertension*, is normal in clinic but high in daily life. Both are real, both are common, and both are why out-of-office averages have become the trusted standard. Home-monitoring hypertension is reckoned at roughly **135/85 mmHg** and a 24-hour ambulatory average at about **130/80 mmHg** — lower thresholds than the office, because relaxed everyday readings sit lower.[^escesh]

Time of day matters too. Pressure follows a daily rhythm: typically lowest during sleep, rising before you wake, peaking in the morning and again in the early evening. This is exactly why the advice is to measure under the same conditions, at the same times, and read the trend rather than any single dot — the same logic that governs [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate).

## When a reading warrants follow-up

<Aside type="caution">
A single high reading is not an emergency — confirm before acting. On a reading of **180 systolic or higher, and/or 120 diastolic or higher**, wait a minute and re-measure. If it stays at or above 180/120 **without** symptoms, this is severe hypertension: contact your health professional promptly. If it is 180/120 or higher **with** chest pain, breathlessness, a neurological or vision change, or a severe headache, treat it as a hypertensive emergency and call emergency services.[^accaha] [^ahaacute]

At the other end, most low blood pressure is harmless. It becomes a concern when it comes with **symptoms** — dizziness, fainting, confusion, blurred vision — or with shock signs such as cold clammy skin and a weak, racing pulse, which need emergency care.[^statpearlshypo]

A steady, symptom-free Stage 2 reading such as 140/90 is not an alert to rush anywhere. It is a reason to book a conversation with a clinician about management — and to keep measuring well in the meantime.
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

Blood pressure rarely moves alone. It tracks body weight and dietary sodium over weeks, so a falling systolic average often shadows sustained weight loss or a lower-salt diet — a likely contributor, not proof of cause. Pulse pressure and arterial stiffness tie the daily number to the slow process of vascular ageing. And pressure is not pulse: a perfectly normal blood pressure alongside a persistently high or irregular [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) still deserves attention on its own terms.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Anchor on your own baseline measured under consistent conditions; use the population bands as a sanity check, not a scoreboard.
