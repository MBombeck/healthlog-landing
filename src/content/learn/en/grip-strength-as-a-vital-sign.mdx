Of all the things that predict how long you will live, one of the strongest is also one of the cheapest to measure: how hard you can squeeze. It sounds like a party trick, but in a study of nearly 140,000 people across seventeen countries, grip strength predicted death from any cause _better than systolic blood pressure did_.[^pure] A hand dynamometer costs less than a month of most gym memberships, takes ten seconds, and reads out a number that tracks something far larger than your forearms. Strength, it turns out, is a vital sign — and grip is the easiest way to read it.

## What a squeeze actually proxies

![](/learn-img-2/grip-strength-as-a-vital-sign.jpg)

Grip strength is not interesting because your hands matter especially. It is interesting because it is a faithful, low-effort proxy for **whole-body strength and muscle quality**.[^bohannon] Maximal grip correlates with strength in the legs and trunk, with total muscle mass, and with the integrity of the neuromuscular system that drives all of it. When that system declines — through ageing, inactivity or illness — grip declines with it, which is why a falling grip is one of the first measurable signs of **sarcopenia**, the age-related loss of muscle mass and function.[^ewgsop] A single number from a hand-held device stands in for a whole-body audit you would otherwise need a lab to run.

## Orientation ranges

Grip is read against your own age and sex, never a fixed target. It rises through early adulthood, plateaus in the thirties, and declines from mid-life onward — so a value that is unremarkable at 30 can be excellent at 70.

| Group                  | Orientation peak (dominant hand) | Clinical low-strength cut-off              |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Men, early adulthood   | ~46–51 kg                        | —                                          |
| Women, early adulthood | ~28–31 kg                        | —                                          |
| Men, ~65–70            | ~35–40 kg                        | under 27 kg (probable sarcopenia)[^ewgsop] |
| Women, ~65–70          | ~21–25 kg                        | under 16 kg (probable sarcopenia)[^ewgsop] |

The peak figures are population orientation from cross-sectional norms; men test substantially higher than women at every age, and absolute values vary with hand size and dynamometer.[^dodds] The clinical cut-offs are different in kind — below ~27 kg for men or ~16 kg for women is the threshold European working groups use to flag _probable sarcopenia_ and prompt further assessment, not a fitness score.[^ewgsop] As with [VO₂max](/learn/vo2max-and-longevity), the honest reading is your percentile within your own band, and your direction within it.

## The mortality link

The headline finding is robust. In the PURE study, every 5 kg drop in grip strength was associated with roughly a 16% higher risk of death from any cause and a 17% higher risk of cardiovascular death — and grip was a _stronger_ predictor of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than systolic blood pressure.[^pure] This is an association, not a mechanism: squeezing harder does not directly extend life. Grip is a window onto the underlying muscular and neurological health that _does_, and onto the frailty that accompanies its loss. That is exactly why it belongs alongside [steps](/learn/steps-and-movement) and cardiorespiratory fitness rather than on its own — strength and aerobic capacity are different axes of the same healthspan.

## Measuring the trend

The value is in the line, not the dot. To make grip comparable over time, standardise the conditions: the same dynamometer, seated with the elbow bent at a right angle, two or three maximal squeezes per hand with a short rest, recording the best.[^bohannon] Test under the same conditions each time and watch the rolling trend across months — a single low reading after a long day or a hand injury says nothing.

<Aside type="note">
  A grip reading dips with fatigue, a recent hard workout, a wrist or hand
  injury, or simply a different device — none of which reflects your underlying
  strength. Log the obvious confounders and compare like with like: same
  dynamometer, same posture, same hand. One low squeeze is noise; a steady
  decline over months is the signal worth acting on.
</Aside>

## How to move the number

Grip — and the whole-body strength it proxies — responds to **resistance training**. The major activity guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activity working all the main muscle groups on at least two days a week, in addition to aerobic activity, and this is the single most reliable lever for building and preserving strength across the lifespan.[^acsm] The age-related decline is partly inevitable and substantially modifiable: trained adults hold strength far later than sedentary ones. You do not train your grip by squeezing a ball — you train it by training your body.

<Aside type="caution">
  A low grip reading is a prompt, not a diagnosis. Below the clinical cut-offs,
  or a clear unexplained decline, is worth raising with a clinician — it can
  reflect sarcopenia, but also nutrition, neurological or joint conditions that
  need proper assessment. If you have been inactive or have a heart, joint or
  other condition, check with a clinician before starting resistance training.
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

It seems implausible that a handshake could foretell anything important, which is precisely why the finding is worth taking seriously: grip is a cheap, fast proxy for the whole-body strength and muscular health that decline quietly with age. Set beside your [cardiorespiratory fitness](/learn/vo2max-and-longevity), your [daily movement](/learn/steps-and-movement) and your [body composition](/learn/beyond-the-scale), a grip trend fills the gap the cardio metrics leave open — and points to the one intervention, resistance training, that the others can't supply.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Grip strength is a population-anchored health indicator read for trend, not a diagnosis; clinical cut-offs flag the need for assessment, not a verdict.
