Most advice about sleep collapses into a single instruction: get your eight hours. It is a reasonable target and a poor headline, because it hides the part that matters. Eight hours snatched at random — late on weeknights, long on weekends, a nap to patch the gap — is not the same as seven on a steady schedule. The body keeps a clock, and the clock cares less about the size of the dose than about when it arrives. The timing is the signal.

## What a night of sleep is actually made of

Sleep is not one state but a cycle the brain repeats four to six times a night, roughly every 90 to 110 minutes.[^statpearls] Each cycle moves through lighter and deeper stages and ends in a stretch of REM, the stage where most vivid dreaming happens. Two things shift across the night: deep sleep is front-loaded into the first cycles, and REM periods lengthen toward morning.[^statpearls] That ordering is why a night cut short at either end loses different things — go to bed late and you trim deep sleep; wake early and you trim REM.

In the laboratory, a healthy adult night divides up roughly like this:

| Stage | Typical share of the night |
| --- | --- |
| N1 (lightest) | ~2–5% |
| N2 | ~45–50% (the largest share) |
| N3 (deep / slow-wave) | ~15–25% |
| REM | ~20–25% |

These proportions are strongly age-dependent — deep sleep is highest in children and adolescents and declines from the twenties onward, while infants spend close to half the night in REM.[^statpearls] None of it is something to optimise stage by stage. The architecture takes care of itself when the schedule is steady and the total is adequate.

## How much adults actually need

The headline number is more honest than it's usually given credit for. National guidance puts adults aged 18 to 64 at **7 to 9 hours** a night, and older adults at **7 to 8**.[^nsf] A joint sleep-medicine consensus settles on a floor: at least 7 hours a night, regularly, for adults — with no upper bound endorsed.[^aasm]

That last phrase matters. The relationship between sleep and health is U-shaped, not "more is always better." Chronically sleeping under seven hours links to obesity, type-2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, weakened immunity, depression and raised all-cause mortality — but habitually very long sleep carries its own associated risk.[^aasm] The lower band for older adults reflects sleep that typically shrinks with age, not a lesser need for the restorative kind. Pregnancy, illness and recovery all push the requirement upward.

## The argument: regularity rivals duration

Here is the claim worth sitting with. How long you sleep matters; *when* you sleep may matter just as much. In a large UK Biobank cohort, the regularity of sleep timing predicted all-cause mortality at least as strongly as duration did — the most regular sleepers carried markedly lower mortality risk than the least regular, even after accounting for how long they slept.[^windred]

The tool behind that finding is the Sleep Regularity Index: the probability that you are in the same state — asleep or awake — at any two moments 24 hours apart, averaged over about a week. It runs from 0 (random) to 100 (perfectly regular), with roughly 94 for a steady sleeper, around 70 for the slightly irregular, and near 34 for the highly irregular.[^phillips] There is no universal good-or-bad cutoff. It is a number to read against yourself over time, not against a scoreboard.

The mechanism is the circadian clock. The body anticipates sleep, releases melatonin, and lowers core temperature on a schedule it learns from your habits. Move the schedule around — the classic "social jet lag" of weekday early starts and weekend lie-ins — and you ask the clock to re-anchor every few days, which it does slowly and grudgingly. Two people can log identical hours; the one who logs them at the same time each night is doing measurably better.

## What a sleep trend can reveal

A single night says little. A line over weeks says a great deal, and on a few axes at once.

- **Duration drifting down** across a busy month is the obvious one — and the debt it builds is real. Sleep debt is a rolling sum of nights that fell short of need, and recovery from it is partial and nonlinear: one long weekend does not fully reverse several restricted nights.[^nsf]
- **Regularity fraying** — bedtimes scattering wider week to week — is the subtler and, on the evidence, possibly the more consequential pattern.[^windred]
- **Efficiency** (time asleep divided by time in bed) sitting comfortably around 85–90% is a healthy average; counter-intuitively, efficiency pushed above ~95% can signal that you are not giving yourself enough time in bed rather than sleeping superbly.[^aasm-manual]

A wrist wearable is genuinely useful for the first two. Read alongside [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate), [HRV](/learn/heart-rate-variability) and [respiratory rate](/learn/respiratory-rate), a sleep trend becomes part of an early-illness and recovery picture rather than a lonely statistic.

<Aside type="note">
Be skeptical of the stage breakdown. Consumer sleep trackers estimate the deep/REM/light split with only fair-to-moderate agreement against clinical polysomnography — the lab standard that records brain waves, eye movement and muscle tone (agreement around kappa 0.21–0.53).[^aasm-cst] Total sleep time and your bedtime are far more trustworthy than any "deep sleep %," and trackers tend to overestimate how long you slept and miss brief waking. Treat the stage figures as a rough personal trend, not a clinical measurement — and remember time in bed is not the same as time asleep.
</Aside>

## When sleep is the symptom

<Aside type="caution">
Most rough nights are just rough nights. A few patterns are worth raising with a clinician rather than tracking. Loud snoring with witnessed pauses in breathing, gasping or choking awakenings, and persistently unrefreshing sleep with pronounced daytime sleepiness can point to a sleep disorder such as obstructive sleep apnoea — a wearable flagging overnight [blood-oxygen](/learn/blood-oxygen-spo2) dips can be an early hint, not a diagnosis. Sudden or severe insomnia, or sleep changes arriving alongside low mood, also deserve assessment. Night-shift work warrants a calm mention rather than alarm: it was classified as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A) in 2019, which reflects the strength of the evidence, not the size of any one person's risk.[^iarc]
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

The practical takeaway is unglamorous and freeing at once: the most effective sleep habit available to most people is not an extra hour but a consistent one. Same bedtime, same wake time, weekends included — bright light in the morning, dim light at night.[^cdc] It costs nothing and it is the one lever the evidence keeps pointing back to.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Anchor on your own baseline — your regularity, your typical hours, your trend — and use the population ranges as a sanity check, not a target to chase.
