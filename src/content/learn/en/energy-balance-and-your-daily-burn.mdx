Every day your body spends energy, and every day you eat some. Set one against the other and you get a single figure, the energy balance, that sounds like the most useful number in nutrition: a deficit and you lose weight, a surplus and you gain. The idea is sound. The figure you can actually compute from a watch and a food log is softer than it looks, because both sides of the equation are estimates, and one of them is usually the larger error. Read with that in mind, a daily balance becomes a genuinely useful trend. Read as a precise ledger, it misleads.

## What your daily burn is made of

![](/learn-img-2/energy-balance-and-your-daily-burn.jpg)

Total daily energy expenditure, often shortened to TDEE, has three parts.[^westerterp] The largest is **basal or resting metabolism**: the energy that keeps you alive at rest, from your heart and brain to your body temperature. Next is the **energy you spend moving**, from a run to carrying shopping or fidgeting at a desk. The smallest is the **thermic effect of food**, the energy your body uses to digest and store what you eat, which comes to roughly 5 to 15 percent of daily expenditure on a mixed diet.[^westerterp]

International guidance expresses total expenditure as a multiple of basal metabolism, the physical activity level. A sedentary or lightly active lifestyle sits at about 1.4 to 1.7 times basal metabolism, an active one at 1.7 to 2.0, and a vigorous one higher still.[^fao] Turned around, that means that for anyone who is not very active, resting metabolism is more than half of everything they burn in a day, and the workout, however hard it felt, is a smaller slice than intuition suggests.

## Estimating resting energy

Measuring resting metabolism properly needs indirect calorimetry: a mouthpiece or hood that measures the oxygen you use. Almost nobody does that at home, so the usual route is an equation built from weight, height, age and sex. The most widely validated is the **Mifflin-St Jeor** equation, developed in 1990 from measurements in healthy adults.[^mifflin] A systematic review of the common equations found it the most reliable of them: it predicted resting metabolism within 10 percent of the measured value in more people, lean and obese, than any other, with the narrowest error range.[^frankenfield]

"More people than any other" is not "everyone". The same review notes that older adults and several ethnic groups were underrepresented in the data the equations were built and tested on.[^frankenfield] For an individual, the equation is a good starting point, not a measurement.

## Active energy from a wearable

The moving part of the burn is where watches and rings come in, and it is the softest number of the lot. In a laboratory study of seven wrist-worn devices, most measured heart rate well, but **none estimated energy expenditure with an error below 20 percent**, and the error was larger during walking and in people with a higher body mass index.[^shcherbina] A later meta-analysis of 60 validation studies came to a similar conclusion: accuracy varies widely with the type of activity, and adding heart rate to motion sensing helps but does not close the gap.[^odriscoll]

Because the error depends on the device and on what you were doing, the active calories from one watch are best compared with the active calories from the same watch. Switching devices can shift your balance overnight with nothing about you having changed.

<Aside type="note">
  A sudden jump in your energy balance after a new watch, a firmware update or
  a different food app is usually the measurement changing, not your body.
  Compare weeks measured the same way, and treat the first week on a new
  device as a new baseline.
</Aside>

## The intake side is the harder one

If active energy is soft, logged intake is often softer. Food logging depends on memory and on guessing portions. In a well-known study of people who reported eating very little yet not losing weight, careful measurement showed that they underreported their food intake by about 47 percent on average and overreported their physical activity by about 51 percent; their metabolism, by contrast, was within 5 percent of what their body composition predicted.[^lichtman] That was a small, specific group, not everyone who keeps a food diary, but the direction of the error is the familiar one: intake tends to be logged low and activity high, which makes a deficit look bigger than it is.

## Why the scale lags the arithmetic

Even a perfectly measured balance would not translate neatly into weight. Expenditure is not fixed. As weight changes, the energy the body spends changes with it, and mathematical models of human metabolism show that weight responds to a sustained change in intake slowly, with half-times of about a year.[^hall] On top of that, the body defends its energy stores after weight loss with what is called adaptive thermogenesis: expenditure falls further than the change in body size alone would explain, which helps explain why keeping weight off is often harder than losing it.[^rosenbaum]

So a steady deficit of a few hundred calories a day does not produce the same loss every week for ever.

## Reading your own balance

Put together, a daily energy balance is most useful when you treat it as a direction rather than a number:

| Question                                    | What the balance can say                                       |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Am I roughly eating what I burn this month? | A weekly average, read against your weight trend               |
| Did one day put me over?                    | Very little: one day's error is larger than most real swings   |
| Why is my weight not moving as predicted?   | Logging gaps, device error and adaptation are the usual causes |
| Is my metabolism broken?                    | It cannot tell you; that needs measurement and a clinician     |

The most honest check on any energy balance is the slow trend of your weight over several weeks. If the balance says deficit and the weight trend is flat, the measurements are disagreeing, and the weight is usually the one to believe.

## How HealthLog reads it

HealthLog's energy balance card takes what you ate from your nutrient data and subtracts resting plus active energy, day by day. It deliberately leaves a day out rather than guess: a day with no food logged has no balance, because a zero would read as a fast, and a day without active energy has none either, because leaving it out would make a deficit look larger than it was. When no resting reading arrived, it estimates resting energy with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation from your profile and says on how many days it did. The headline is a seven-day average, and the card shows the numbers without telling you what they should be.
