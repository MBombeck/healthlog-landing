Water is the medium your body runs in. It makes up well over half of an adult's body mass, and almost every process you depend on — moving blood, regulating temperature, cushioning joints, carrying nutrients in and waste out — happens in or through it. Which is exactly why a topic that sounds trivial turns out to be one of the most over-simplified in everyday health advice. The truthful version is less tidy than "drink eight glasses," and more useful.

## What fluid balance actually does

![](/learn-img-2/hydration-and-your-body.jpg)

Your body holds its water content within a remarkably tight range, balancing what comes in (drinks, food, and a little from metabolism) against what goes out (urine, sweat, breath, and stool). The kidneys do most of the fine adjustment, concentrating or diluting urine on demand, while the hormone vasopressin and your sense of thirst nudge intake in the right direction.[^iom] When you take in a little extra, you simply pass a little more; when you're short, your body conserves. This is a control system, not a fuel tank you must keep topped to the brim.

That system is why the scale lies to you about water. Day-to-day swings of a kilogram or two are overwhelmingly fluid — a salty meal, a hard workout, glycogen stores, where you are in a monthly cycle — not fat gained or lost overnight. Body fat changes slowly; water moves fast. It's the single biggest reason to read [the trend, not the daily number](/learn/beyond-the-scale).

## Why hydration shows up in your heart rate and your mood

When you lose enough fluid that blood volume drops, the heart compensates by beating faster to keep circulation steady — so dehydration tends to nudge [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) and exertion heart rate upward.[^iom] That same fluid loss is why a hot day or a long run can leave your pulse running higher than usual at the same effort.

The effects you *feel* arrive earlier than most people expect. Controlled studies find that mild fluid deficits — on the order of 1–2% of body mass — are enough to dent mood, raise the sense of effort, and impair concentration and short-term memory, with headache and fatigue common at the milder end.[^armstrong][^ganio] None of this requires dramatic dehydration. It's the ordinary "off" feeling of an afternoon when you simply haven't had much to drink.

## The honest truth about "eight glasses a day"

There is no strong scientific basis for a universal "drink eight 8-ounce glasses a day" rule. Reviews tracing the claim have been unable to find credible evidence behind it, and note it likely came from misreading older guidance that *also* counted the water already in food.[^valtin] Authoritative bodies don't set a single fixed target either: they give population reference values and stress that needs vary widely with body size, activity, climate, and diet.

| Reference value (total water, all sources) | Adult women | Adult men |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EFSA adequate intake[^efsa] | ~2.0 L/day | ~2.5 L/day |
| U.S. National Academies (IOM) adequate intake[^iom] | ~2.7 L/day | ~3.7 L/day |

Two things make those numbers far less prescriptive than they look. First, they're *total water* — and roughly a fifth of it typically comes from food, with the rest from drinks of every kind, not just plain water.[^efsa] Second, they describe what populations tend to consume in good health, not a quota each person must hit.

So what should an ordinary person actually do? For most healthy people on an ordinary day, the body's own signals are reasonable guides:

- **Thirst** is a real, functioning sensor. Drinking to thirst keeps the vast majority of healthy adults adequately hydrated.[^iom]
- **Urine colour** is a rough at-home check: pale straw suggests you're fine; consistently dark, strong-smelling urine suggests you could drink more. (Vitamin supplements and some foods tint urine independently, so it's a guide, not a gauge.)

The common claims that coffee or tea "don't count" and "dehydrate you" are overstated: the fluid in caffeinated drinks still contributes to your daily total, and any mild diuretic effect doesn't cancel it out for habitual drinkers.[^efsa]

## When fluid balance matters more

The "listen to your body" approach has limits, and they're worth knowing. Thirst lags behind real need during heavy sweating, and it blunts with age, so older adults can be genuinely short of fluid before they feel it. Three situations deserve deliberate attention rather than passive thirst:

- **Heat.** High temperature and humidity drive large, sometimes unnoticed sweat losses.
- **Exercise.** Prolonged or intense effort, especially in warmth, can outpace thirst — weighing yourself before and after a long session shows how much you actually lost.
- **Illness.** Fever, vomiting, and diarrhoea raise losses sharply and are the classic route to dehydration, particularly in children and older adults.

<Aside type="caution">
More is not automatically better. Drinking far beyond need — most often during endurance events — can dilute blood sodium into a dangerous condition called **hyponatraemia**, whose early signs (headache, nausea, confusion) overlap with dehydration, which is why "just drink more" is the wrong reflex when something feels off during prolonged exertion.[^iom] Separately, anyone with heart, kidney, or liver disease, or taking medication that affects fluid balance, may have a deliberately *restricted* target — follow clinical advice over any general rule. A sudden, large, unexplained change in thirst or urine volume is itself worth a conversation with a clinician.
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

Hydration isn't a number to chase so much as a balance your body already manages well. Watch it where it actually shows up — the [weight that swings with fluid, not fat](/learn/beyond-the-scale), a [heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) that climbs when you're short, a [body-temperature](/learn/body-temperature-baseline) reading taken in the heat, the energy and [steadier blood sugar](/learn/blood-sugar-beyond-diabetes) of a day you've eaten and drunk well — and the daily question mostly answers itself.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual needs vary widely. Use thirst and urine colour as everyday guides, pay closer attention in heat, exercise, and illness, and defer to a clinician's advice if you have a condition that affects fluid balance.
