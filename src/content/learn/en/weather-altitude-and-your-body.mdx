You wake up, glance at last night's numbers, and something is off — resting heart rate up a few beats, sleep choppy, breathing a little quicker. The instinct is to look inward: bad night, coming down with something, overdid it yesterday. Often the answer is in the room, or outside the window. Heat, altitude and the air you breathed all push on the same physiology your wearable is reading, and they do it before you consciously notice. A figure that looks like a personal red flag is sometimes just your body responding, correctly, to its environment.

## Your numbers aren't only about you

![](/learn-img-2/weather-altitude-and-your-body.jpg)

Every metric you track is the output of a system doing its job in a particular set of conditions. The same logic that says to read [trends, not single numbers](/learn/reading-your-trends) — and to keep measurement conditions constant — has a corollary: when the _environment_ changes, the numbers will too, and that shift is information, not malfunction. Heat, thin air and polluted air are three of the most common confounders behind an unexplained morning reading, and each moves your data in a fairly predictable direction.

## Heat: thermoregulation has a heart-rate cost

When you are warm, your body protects its core temperature by sending blood to the skin to shed heat. That redistribution means the heart has to pump harder and faster to keep circulation steady, so a hot day or a hot bedroom lifts both resting and exertion [heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate), often by several beats, with no change in fitness whatsoever.[^cdc] Heat also taxes sleep directly: falling asleep depends on your core temperature _dropping_, and a warm room blunts that fall, fragmenting the night and nudging the next morning's readings.[^cdc] None of that is alarming on a single warm night. Sustained heat strain is a different matter — it is a genuine medical risk, and the body's compensations have limits.

<Aside type="caution">
  Heat illness is a real emergency, not just a confounder. Heavy sweating,
  cramps, dizziness or nausea signal heat exhaustion; a high body temperature
  with confusion, a rapid pulse, or hot dry skin signals heat stroke, which is
  life-threatening and needs emergency care immediately. Older adults, young
  children, and people with heart conditions or on certain medications are at
  higher risk. A wearable cannot diagnose any of this — act on how you feel and
  the heat around you.
</Aside>

## Altitude: less oxygen, more compensation

Go up high enough and the air holds the same fraction of oxygen but at lower pressure, so each breath delivers less. Your body compensates immediately: [blood oxygen](/learn/blood-oxygen-spo2) falls — around 90% can be entirely normal at ~2,500 m — while [breathing rate](/learn/respiratory-rate) and heart rate rise to move more air and circulate it faster.[^spo2] Over the following days, acclimatisation kicks in and the numbers partly settle. This is why a wrist SpO₂ that would worry you at sea level can be unremarkable on a mountain holiday, and why your resting heart rate runs high for the first nights at altitude.

The flip side is that altitude has a danger zone of its own. Ascending too fast can cause acute mountain sickness, so the standard advice above roughly 2,500 m is to climb gradually and give the body time.[^altitude]

<Aside type="note">
  Above ~2,500 m, expect a lower SpO₂, a higher resting heart rate and faster
  breathing for the first few days — that is acclimatisation, not a fault in
  your device or your heart. What is not normal is a worsening headache with
  nausea, breathlessness at rest, or confusion: those are warning signs of
  altitude illness and a reason to stop ascending and descend. Ascend gradually
  and don't read your sea-level baselines onto a mountain.
</Aside>

## Air quality: the input you can't feel

Fine particulate matter — PM2.5, particles small enough to reach deep into the lungs and cross into the bloodstream — is the environmental input you are least likely to notice and that still moves your physiology. Exposure is linked to higher cardiorespiratory strain: raised inflammation, higher blood pressure, and added load on the heart and lungs, with risk that climbs across the whole exposure range rather than switching on at a line.[^pm25][^aha] The WHO's air-quality guidelines set the annual PM2.5 reference at 5 µg/m³ and the 24-hour level at 15 µg/m³ — values much of the world routinely exceeds.[^pm25] On a high-pollution day, a quicker breathing rate or a poorer recovery score can have an outdoor cause, especially after exercising in it.

## Reading your data against the world, not in a vacuum

Put together, these three explain a large share of "off" mornings. The practical move is to log the context — a heatwave, a trip to altitude, a smoky or smoggy day — the same way you would log a late dinner or a hard workout, so a single environment-driven reading doesn't get mistaken for a developing problem. The signal worth attending to is still a _sustained_ drift that holds once the environment returns to normal.

| Environment                   | Typical direction of your readings                                                        |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Heat / hot bedroom            | Resting & exertion heart rate up; sleep more fragmented[^cdc]                             |
| Altitude (first days)         | SpO₂ down; breathing rate & heart rate up; settles with acclimatisation[^spo2][^altitude] |
| High PM2.5 / poor air quality | Higher cardiorespiratory strain, especially with outdoor exertion[^pm25][^aha]            |

## The bigger picture

A health metric describes a body _in a place_, and the place leaves fingerprints all over the data. Heat raises your heart rate and breaks up your sleep; altitude drops your oxygen and lifts your pulse; polluted air strains the heart and lungs you can't feel it touching. Reading your [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate), [respiratory rate](/learn/respiratory-rate) and [sleep](/learn/sleep-consistency) with one eye on the environment is what separates a meaningful trend from a heatwave — and it is the same habit, [trend over snapshot](/learn/reading-your-trends), that makes any of these numbers trustworthy.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Environmental effects are context for your readings, not diagnoses — heat illness and altitude illness are medical emergencies to act on by how you feel, not by a wearable.
