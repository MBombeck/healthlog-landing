When a clinician asks about your last period, it isn't small talk. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists asks doctors to treat the menstrual cycle the way they treat pulse, blood pressure and temperature — as a vital sign, a number worth checking at every visit.[^acog] The logic is the same as for any vital sign: it's a recurring rhythm that holds steady when things are well, which is exactly why a shift in it carries information. The cycle is regular enough to recognise a pattern, and personal enough that the pattern is yours.

## The four phases, in order

![](/learn-img-2/the-cycle-as-a-vital-sign.jpg)

The cycle is a single hormonal sequence that runs through four phases, driven by the rise and fall of estrogen and progesterone.[^statpearls][^nichd]

- **Menstrual** — estrogen and progesterone are both low, and the uterine lining sheds. This is the bleeding most people count as the start of the cycle.
- **Follicular** — follicle-stimulating hormone recruits follicles in the ovary, and rising estrogen rebuilds the lining.
- **Ovulatory** — estrogen peaks, triggering a surge of luteinising hormone; ovulation follows roughly 36 to 44 hours after that surge begins.
- **Luteal** — the spent follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which produces progesterone. Progesterone matures the lining and nudges basal [body temperature](/learn/body-temperature-baseline) up by a few tenths of a degree.

That small temperature step is why the cycle and your other vital signs are not really separate readings. [Resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) and heart-rate variability often shift across the luteal phase too, which is useful to know before you read a baseline deviation as something it isn't.

## What "normal" looks like — and why two ranges both count

The honest answer here is two numbers, not one, because two reputable bodies publish slightly different ranges that answer slightly different questions.

| Measure | Range | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adult cycle length (patient-facing) | 21–35 days | NICHD/NIH, 2024 |
| Adult cycle length (clinical "vital sign") | 24–38 days | ACOG, 2015 |
| Adolescent cycle | wider, more variable | ACOG, 2015 |
| Bleeding duration | about 5 days, up to roughly 7 | ACOG, 2015 |
| Luteal phase | about 12–14 days | StatPearls, 2023 |

The patient-facing 21–35 day band describes the everyday spread of healthy adult cycles.[^nichd] The clinical 24–38 day band is the screening frame a doctor uses to decide whether something needs a closer look.[^acog] Neither is wrong; they're drawn for different purposes, and it's worth seeing both rather than memorising one.

A cycle measured from the first day of one period to the first day of the next isn't expected to be identical month to month. Variation of about a week is ordinary — roughly 46% of women aged 18 to 40 see cycle-to-cycle swings of seven days or more.[^nichd] The luteal phase is the relatively constant anchor; most of the variation in total length lives in the follicular phase.

## What shifts the rhythm

Plenty of ordinary life moves the cycle. Age is the biggest factor at both ends — adolescence and perimenopause are both more variable by design. Significant changes in body weight or training load, sustained stress, illness, thyroid and other endocrine conditions, hormonal contraception, and pregnancy all change length or regularity. As with any vital sign, a single unusual cycle rarely means much on its own. A *sustained* change from your own established pattern is the more informative signal — which is the whole reason tracking your personal baseline beats comparing yourself to a population range.

## The honest limit: prediction is approximate

This is where the cycle resists the tidy story an app likes to tell. Ovulation is best estimated by counting *back* from your next expected period, never fixed at "day 14." The fertile window is the six days ending on the day of ovulation — the five days before, plus ovulation day itself.[^wilcox] But its timing is genuinely variable even in people with regular cycles: in only about 30% of women does that window fall entirely within the textbook days 10 to 17.[^wilcox]

Calendar prediction needs at least three recorded cycles — ideally six to twelve — and stays approximate even then. Some signals firm it up. An at-home LH test predicts ovulation about 12 to 36 hours ahead; a basal-temperature rise and a cervical-mucus peak confirm it only after the fact. The symptothermal method, which double-checks mucus against basal temperature, is the recognised fertility-awareness gold standard.[^statpearls]

<Aside type="caution">
Algorithmic cycle predictions — from any app, wearable or device — are **not a contraceptive method**. Population-default predictions are unreliable, individual timing varies widely even in regular cyclers, and perimenopause makes cycles unpredictable again. Anyone using fertility awareness to prevent or plan a pregnancy should use a validated, properly instructed method, not a single predicted date.
</Aside>

## When a change is worth a conversation

Most month-to-month variation is noise. A handful of patterns are worth raising with a clinician — and these are signals that route you *to* a doctor, not something to interpret or diagnose yourself.

<Aside type="caution">
Raise it with a clinician when cycles consistently fall outside the typical range — recurring intervals shorter than roughly 21 days or longer than about 45 days — or when there is a marked, sustained change from your own established pattern.[^acog]

Absent periods also warrant evaluation: any single gap of 90 days or more (about three months) in someone who previously menstruated, or a first period that has not arrived by age 15.[^acog]

Bleeding counts as heavy or prolonged when it lasts more than 7 days, or soaks through a pad or tampon more often than every one to two hours.[^acog]

Bleeding between periods or after intercourse is worth reporting. Seek urgent care for bleeding accompanied by light-headedness, dizziness, a racing pulse or chest pain.[^acog]
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

Treated as a vital sign, the cycle rewards the same habit as the others: measure it consistently, watch the line rather than the dot, and read it alongside the signals it travels with. The luteal temperature step ties it to [body temperature](/learn/body-temperature-baseline); the luteal shift in [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) ties it to your cardiovascular baseline; stress and [sleep](/learn/sleep-consistency) feed into all three. On its own, the cycle is a personal rhythm worth knowing by heart. In context, it's one more honest early-warning system you already carry with you.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Lead with your own pattern; use the population ranges only as a secondary sanity band.
