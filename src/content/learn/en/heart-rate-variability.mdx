No metric in consumer health has been hyped harder, or understood worse, than heart rate variability. It gets sold as a single readiness score, a daily verdict on whether you're "recovered," a number to chase upward. Almost none of that survives contact with how the measurement actually behaves. HRV is genuinely useful — it is one of the few non-invasive windows onto the nervous system you can read at home — but only if you treat it as a personal trend, not a leaderboard, and only if you respect how much the measurement conditions move it.

## What HRV actually measures

![](/learn-img-2/heart-rate-variability.jpg)

A healthy heart does not tick like a metronome. Even at a steady pulse, the gap between one beat and the next varies by milliseconds, and that beat-to-beat variation is what HRV captures. The fluctuation reflects the balance of your autonomic nervous system: the sympathetic "accelerator" and the parasympathetic "brake," the latter carried largely by the vagus nerve. When the brake is doing its job — at rest, well recovered — the spacing between beats becomes more variable. When the body is under load, that variability flattens.

The way the variation gets quantified was standardised by an international task force back in 1996.[^escnaspe] Two families of measures matter. **Time-domain** measures include SDNN, which captures overall variability, and RMSSD, which tracks parasympathetic (vagal) tone — RMSSD is the measure most overnight wrist readings approximate. **Frequency-domain** measures split the signal into spectral bands. You don't need the math; you need to know that more than one number wears the name "HRV," and they are not interchangeable.

## Why there is no "normal" HRV

This is the part the hype skips. Between-person variation in HRV dwarfs within-person variation. Your healthy baseline might be a third of someone else's healthy baseline, and both are fine. Comparing your number to a friend's, or to an online "average," is close to meaningless.[^escnaspe]

The orientation figures below exist only to show the scale, and to show why one device's reading cannot be read against another's:

| Measure | Typical value | Recording type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short-term RMSSD (~5-min, lying still) | ~42 ± 15 ms | clinical, supine |
| Short-term SDNN (~5-min, lying still) | ~50 ± 16 ms | clinical, supine |
| 24-hour SDNN, healthy | over 100 ms | full-day Holter |
| Nightly RMSSD, teens and 20s | ~55–105 ms | overnight wearable |
| Nightly RMSSD, 60 and over | ~25–45 ms | overnight wearable |

Two things jump out. First, HRV falls steeply with age — a drop of more than half across the adult decades.[^umetani] Second, a five-minute clinical recording, a 24-hour average and an overnight wrist reading are three different measurements that happen to share a name.[^escnaspe] A 24-hour SDNN normative figure sits near 141 ms (±39); a morning wearable number is nowhere near that, and nothing has gone wrong. Recording length, posture, breathing rate and time of day each shift the value, and absolute numbers don't transfer across devices.

## Conditions dominate the reading

HRV is exquisitely sensitive to how and when it's taken. Posture changes it. Breathing rate changes it — slow breathing alone can inflate the number. Time of day, the last meal, caffeine, illness, sleep and acute stress all move it.[^escnaspe] Alcohol suppresses HRV in a dose-dependent way, strongest the night of and the morning after, often while heart rate and blood pressure still look perfectly normal.[^quintana] Acute mental stress lowers it through vagal withdrawal.[^thayer]

The practical consequence: a reading taken under inconsistent conditions tells you about the conditions, not about you. This is why the standard advice is to let a device measure overnight, in the same position, across the same window every night — and then to ignore any single result.

## Read the trend, not the night

Single-night swings of about ±10% are routine and mean nothing on their own.[^plews] The honest unit of analysis is a rolling average — a seven-day line, not last night's dot.

Sports science has a useful frame here. The smallest worthwhile change is roughly half the within-person standard deviation of your log-transformed baseline; moves smaller than that are noise. A rising coefficient of variation paired with a falling mean points toward accumulated fatigue or overreaching.[^plews] These are athlete-monitoring conventions — informational, never a clinical alarm. The signal worth watching is never a bad night. It's a *sustained* drift below your own established baseline.

<Aside type="note">
A one-night HRV dip after a late dinner, a glass of wine, a hard workout or a stressful day is expected and recovers on its own. Logging the obvious confounders — alcohol, training, poor sleep — is what lets you tell a meaningful decline from yesterday's pizza. The number is only as smart as the context around it.
</Aside>

## What HRV is not

Two popular interpretations don't hold up, and it's worth naming them.

The **LF/HF ratio** is widely sold as a measure of "sympathovagal balance" or a stress score. It is not. Low-frequency power is not a clean sympathetic marker, and the ratio rests on an oversimplified linear model of how the two systems interact.[^billman] Prefer high-frequency power or RMSSD as the vagal-tone signal, and treat any "stress" readout derived from the ratio as a proxy, not a measurement.

**Recovery, readiness and stress scores** are device-specific composites — overnight HRV trend blended with resting heart rate, sleep, respiration and temperature, scored against your own rolling baseline. They have no clinical reference range, are not standardised across devices, and their inputs are often non-independent: a poor night lowers HRV *and* worsens the sleep input, so the same stressor gets counted twice. Read them as a wellness nudge, not a diagnosis.

## When it actually points somewhere

On its own, HRV is not a red-flag metric — its day-to-day swing is too large and too individual to alarm on. Its value at the edge of safety is as one voice in a chorus. A sustained drop below your personal baseline, *together with* a rising [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) and a higher overnight [respiratory rate](/learn/respiratory-rate), is a soft early-warning of illness or overreach — a pattern that preceded symptomatic infection in a meaningful fraction of cases in pre-symptomatic studies.[^mishra] Concordance of two or three signals is the message; any one of them alone is not.

<Aside type="caution">
HRV is a wellness signal, not a diagnostic test, and a low number must never escalate to a clinical alarm on its own. There is one hard exception: standard HRV interpretation breaks down entirely in the presence of atrial fibrillation or frequent ectopic (extra) beats, because the rhythm itself is irregular.[^escnaspe] If a wearable repeatedly flags an irregular rhythm, or if a falling HRV trend comes with symptoms — breathlessness, light-headedness, chest discomfort, palpitations — that is a reason to see a clinician, not to refresh the app.
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

HRV is the parasympathetic counterpart to resting heart rate: as recovery degrades, variability falls while the resting rate climbs, and read together with [sleep](/learn/sleep-consistency) they describe how well your body is coping with the load you're putting on it. Lead with your own baseline. Give it a couple of weeks of consistent overnight readings before you trust the line at all, watch the seven-day trend rather than the night, and let the population figures be a distant sanity check — never a scoreboard.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Anchor on your own baseline; the orientation ranges are context, not a target to hit.
