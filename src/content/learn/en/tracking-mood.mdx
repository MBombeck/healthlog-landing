Most health metrics arrive with a unit attached — beats per minute, millimetres of mercury, hours asleep. Mood doesn't. It feels too soft, too personal, too tangled up with the day's events to belong on the same dashboard as your heart rate. But the thing that makes mood seem unmeasurable — that it's a private, in-the-moment read on how you're doing — is exactly what makes a steady log of it valuable. One entry is an opinion. A few hundred entries are a trend, and a trend is data.

## What a mood log actually captures

![](/learn-img-2/tracking-mood.jpg)

A daily mood entry is a subjective self-report of your affective state, usually on a simple ordinal scale such as 1 to 5.[^who5] On its own, a single number is fast to record and easy to dismiss. Its worth comes from repetition: log it for weeks and patterns surface that no single day reveals — the slow drift down across a stressful stretch, the reliable lift after exercise, the dip that arrives on a predictable schedule.[^who5]

Alongside the freeform scale sit several structured instruments that turn the same self-report into something more rigorous. The WHO-5 Well-Being Index, the PHQ-9 (depression), the GAD-7 (anxiety) and the PSS-10 (perceived stress) are validated, repeatable questionnaires with published scoring.[^who5][^phq9] The daily scale is good for spotting movement; the validated screens give you a structured severity measure you can track over time and hand to a clinician.

## Why the trend beats the single entry

Self-report is sensitive to the time of day, recent events, how well you slept, and even how the question is framed.[^pss] That sensitivity is precisely why a one-off reading is noisy and why consistency is the single most useful habit: same scale, same time of day, every day. Measured that way, the day-to-day wobble averages out and the underlying direction becomes legible.

The validated screens build this idea in. For the WHO-5, a fall of 10 percentage points or more counts as a clinically meaningful change — the instrument is designed to be read as movement against your own earlier scores, not as a verdict from a single sitting.[^who5] The same logic applies to a homemade 1-to-5 scale: a week that runs a point lower than your usual baseline says more than any single bad morning.

## How mood interacts with the rest of your data

Mood rarely moves alone, which is what makes it worth logging next to everything else you track.

| Signal | How it tends to move with mood |
| --- | --- |
| Sleep | A run of short or irregular nights often precedes a dip; a low mood in turn disrupts sleep, so the two reinforce each other in both directions.[^who5] |
| Physical activity | Exercise is one of the more reliable same-day and next-day lifts in most people's logs.[^who5] |
| Menstrual cycle | Mood often follows cycle phase, so a recurring monthly dip reads as a pattern rather than a mystery once both are tracked together.[^who5] |
| Stress and life load | Acute stress, alcohol and heavy workloads all show up; perceived stress is a continuous construct that tracks alongside mood rather than a switch that flips.[^pss] |

A poor night's [sleep](/learn/sleep-consistency) or a high-stress day is a plausible contributor to a low entry — not proof of anything clinical, but a useful piece of context. The same data that powers a [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) or [HRV](/learn/heart-rate-variability) baseline becomes far more informative when a mood line runs beside it, and a recurring dip that lines up with [the menstrual cycle](/learn/the-cycle-as-a-vital-sign) is one of the clearest correlations a combined log can surface.

## Reading the validated screens

If you use the structured questionnaires, the published thresholds are worth knowing — and worth treating as screens, never as conclusions.

| Instrument | Action threshold |
| --- | --- |
| WHO-5 (0–100%) | At or below 50% (raw ≤12) indicates poor wellbeing and warrants further screening.[^who5] |
| PHQ-9 (0–27) | A total of 10 or above is the standard action threshold (about 88% sensitivity and specificity for major depression).[^phq9] |
| GAD-7 (0–21) | A total of 10 or above flags probable generalised anxiety disorder (about 89% sensitivity, 82% specificity).[^gad7] |
| PSS-10 (0–40) | No clinical cut-off by design — perceived stress is a continuous construct, read for trend.[^pss] |

These instruments are concordant and well established, but a positive screen is a prompt to talk to a professional, not a diagnosis you can make at home.[^phq9] A single-item daily scale is not a validated diagnostic tool at all — read it for trend only.

## When a low mood warrants reaching out

Most dips are ordinary. Bad weeks happen, and a log that wanders down for a few days and recovers is doing exactly what it should. The pattern worth acting on is a low mood that *persists* — a downward trend in your own scores that holds for two weeks or more, a WHO-5 that settles at or below 50%, or a PHQ-9 or GAD-7 that crosses 10 and stays there.[^who5][^phq9] Depression is common and treatable; reaching out early tends to make it easier to address, not harder.[^who]

<Aside type="caution">
A mood log is a way to notice things sooner, not a diagnostic tool — it can flag a trend, but it cannot tell you what's causing it. If low mood, hopelessness, or loss of interest persists for two weeks or more, or starts getting in the way of everyday life, that's a reason to talk to a doctor or a mental-health professional. You don't need to wait until things feel severe, and reaching out is not an overreaction. And any thought of harming yourself or that you'd be better off dead — even occasional, and whatever an overall score might say — is on its own a reason to reach out right now: contact your local emergency number or a crisis service immediately. These feelings are treatable, and help is available. This page cannot replace urgent support.
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

The objection to tracking mood — that it's just a feeling — is also the point. Feelings are real signals about how the rest of your life and your body are treating you, and a feeling logged consistently stops being a fleeting impression and becomes a line you can read. Kept beside your sleep, your activity and your cycle, a simple mood scale is often the first place a problem shows up, and the easiest place to catch it early.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. A daily scale is for trend; the validated screens are repeatable severity measures, not diagnoses — positive results should prompt professional evaluation.
