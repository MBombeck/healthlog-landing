A wrist wearable hands you a confident stream of numbers — heart rate, sleep stages, blood oxygen, a "recovery" score — and the polish of the display can make all of it feel equally precise. It isn't. Behind the dashboard sit a small handful of physical sensors, each measuring one narrow thing well and a great deal else only by inference. Knowing which is which is the difference between trusting a device too much and getting real value from it.

## Optical heart rate: light bounced off blood

![](/learn-img-2/how-wearables-measure-you.jpg)

The green light glowing on the underside of a wrist band is the heart of most wearables. The technique is **photoplethysmography**, or PPG: an LED shines light into the skin, blood absorbs some of it, and a photodetector reads how much bounces back. Because blood volume in the small vessels rises and falls with each heartbeat, that reflected signal pulses — and counting the pulses gives heart rate.[^ppg]

At rest, this works well. Validation studies repeatedly find wrist PPG tracking a chest electrical signal closely when the wearer is still.[^ppg] The trouble starts with movement. During exercise the band shifts against the skin, muscle and tendon motion contaminates the optical signal, and the device has to guess harder — which is why wrist heart rate during intervals or weightlifting is the least reliable reading most people own.[^ppg] A chest strap, which measures the heart's electrical activity directly rather than optically, stays accurate through motion and remains the practical reference for hard training.[^ppg]

Fit and physiology matter too. A loose band, cold fingers, dense tattoo ink over the sensor, and darker skin tones all change how light travels and can degrade the signal.[^ppg] None of this makes the number useless — it means the *conditions* of a reading are part of the reading.

## The accelerometer: steps, motion, and "sleep"

Almost everything that isn't optical comes from a tiny **accelerometer** — a chip that senses motion in three axes. Step counts come from recognising the rhythmic acceleration pattern of walking, which is why steps are reasonably good on a brisk walk and noisier when you push a stroller, wash dishes, or sit in a bumpy car.

Sleep is the more interesting stretch. A wearable does not see your brain. It infers sleep from how little you move, often combined with heart rate and its beat-to-beat variation, then labels the night into "light," "deep," and "REM" stages.[^sleep] The clinical reference — polysomnography — records brain waves, eye movement, and muscle tone in a lab. Against that standard, consumer devices are fairly good at the simple question of *asleep or awake* and considerably weaker at *which stage*, where staging accuracy drops off.[^sleep] Read the bedtime, wake time, and total duration with some confidence; treat the coloured stage breakdown as an estimate, not a verdict. (For what actually moves the needle, see [sleep consistency](/learn/sleep-consistency).)

## Skin temperature and SpO₂

Newer bands add a **skin-temperature** sensor. It does not report core body temperature; it reports a *deviation* from your own recent nightly baseline. That framing is the point — a relative shift of a fraction of a degree across several nights can hint at illness, the menstrual cycle, or a hard day, while the absolute number means little on its own. (More in [body-temperature baseline](/learn/body-temperature-baseline).)

Blood-oxygen estimation reuses the optical trick with a twist. **Pulse oximetry** shines two wavelengths — red and infrared — because oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin absorb them differently; the ratio yields an estimated SpO₂.[^spo2] Even medical fingertip oximeters carry a margin of error of a couple of percentage points, and wrist-based estimates are looser still.[^spo2] Critically, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has warned that pulse oximeters can read *higher* than true oxygen levels, and that this overestimation is more pronounced in people with darker skin — a limitation first widely documented in hospital data and one that wrist devices inherit.[^fda][^sjoding] Consumer wrist SpO₂ is a wellness feature, not a diagnostic one. (See [blood oxygen (SpO₂)](/learn/blood-oxygen-spo2).)

## Derived scores: estimates built on estimates

The headline numbers — VO₂max, "recovery," "readiness," "strain" — are not measured at all. They are **models**: algorithms that combine the raw sensor streams above with your age, weight, and history to produce a single figure.

Wrist VO₂max is the clearest example. True VO₂max requires a mask, a treadmill, and analysis of the gas you breathe. A wearable estimates it from the relationship between your heart rate and your pace during outdoor activity — a reasonable proxy that tracks fitness changes over time, but an estimate that inherits every error in the heart-rate and GPS data feeding it.[^vo2] "Recovery" and "readiness" scores are proprietary blends, typically leaning on overnight [heart rate variability](/learn/heart-rate-variability), [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate), and sleep — useful as a daily nudge, but not a clinical measurement of anything.

<Aside type="note">
Most of what a wearable shows you is an **estimate, not a lab measurement**. Optical heart rate, inferred sleep stages, wrist SpO₂, and every "score" are models built on a few real signals — accurate enough to reveal *change over time*, rarely precise enough to treat a single value as ground truth. The honest way to read them is as relative, not absolute.
</Aside>

## Trust the trend, know the limits

The common thread across every sensor is the same lesson that runs through [reading your trends](/learn/reading-your-trends): a wearable is far better at detecting *change against your own baseline* than at reporting an exact value. A resting heart rate that climbs five beats over three mornings, a skin temperature that drifts up before a cold, a recovery score that sags after a bad week — these directional signals are where the value lives, because consistent conditions cancel out a lot of measurement error.

| Reading | What it really is | Where accuracy degrades |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heart rate | Optical (PPG) at rest; weaker in motion | Movement, loose fit, tattoos, skin tone |
| Steps | Accelerometer pattern-matching | Non-walking arm motion, vehicles |
| Sleep stages | Inferred from motion + heart rate | Stage labels far less reliable than duration |
| Skin temperature | Deviation from personal baseline | Not core temperature; relative only |
| SpO₂ | Red/infrared absorption ratio | Can over-read; worse with darker skin, motion |
| VO₂max / recovery | Modelled estimates | Inherit every upstream sensor error |

So calibrate your expectations to the sensor. Use the device for direction and habit, lean on a chest strap or a clinician's instrument when you need precision, and let any single alarming number prompt a second look rather than a conclusion.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Anchor on your own baseline; use the numbers as a sanity check, not a scoreboard.
