There is no single "normal" body temperature. There's an average — and there's yours, which is rarely the same thing and which moves on a schedule through the day. Treating 37 °C as a fixed set point and anything else as a problem is one of the oldest small errors in health. A thermometer measures a moment; what's actually useful is knowing where your own thermal baseline sits, and recognising when the reading has drifted off it.

## Where 37 °C came from — and why it's too high

![](/learn-img-2/body-temperature-baseline.jpg)

The familiar 37.0 °C (98.6 °F) figure is an *average*, set down by a 19th-century physician and carried forward ever since. Modern data has nudged it down. A 2019 systematic review put the population mean at **36.59 °C**, with a normal oral range of roughly **35.7–37.4 °C** — wide enough that two perfectly healthy people can sit nearly two degrees apart.[^jgim] So a reading of 36.7 °C is not "below normal"; it's squarely within it. Normal is a distribution, and where you fall in it is personal.

Two things explain most of the spread: who is measuring, and *how*. Your reading depends heavily on the measurement site, and the most common mistake is comparing across sites — a forehead reading one day against an oral reading the next.

| Where it's measured | Approximate reading |
| --- | --- |
| Rectal (closest to core) | ~37.04 °C |
| Tympanic (ear) | ~36.64 °C |
| Oral | ~36.57 °C |
| Axillary (armpit) | ~35.97 °C |

Site differences run up to about 1 °C.[^jgim] The practical rule is simple: pick one site, tag it, and only ever compare like with like.

## The daily rhythm you're built around

Temperature is not steady through the day — it follows a circadian curve, swinging about **0.5 °C** between its lowest and highest points. The nadir lands in the small hours, roughly **04:00–08:00**; the peak comes in the early evening, around **18:00–20:00**.[^jgim] An evening reading that looks slightly "high" is often just the normal diurnal peak, and a chilly-feeling early-morning low is the body at the bottom of its own curve.

This is exactly why a single number tells you so little out of context. The same person, well and unbothered, will read meaningfully differently at 6 a.m. and at 7 p.m. A baseline worth trusting is built from readings taken at the same time of day, at the same site, under the same conditions — much as a [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) is only meaningful measured under steady conditions.

## The luteal rise

In menstruating people there's a second, slower rhythm laid over the daily one. Basal body temperature sits lower through the follicular phase, then sustains a rise of about **0.3–0.5 °C** through the luteal phase, driven by progesterone after ovulation.[^acog] It's a real, repeatable shift — large enough to swamp a careful baseline if the cycle isn't accounted for.

One nuance matters: that rise *confirms* ovulation has already happened. It is a retrospective marker, not a forecast — it tells you where you've been, not what's about to occur. Read alongside [the menstrual cycle](/learn/the-cycle-as-a-vital-sign) as a whole, the temperature shift is one of the clearest windows onto the body's hormonal phase.

## Skin temperature is a deviation, not a thermometer

Many wearable sensors now report an overnight "skin temperature." What that number is, and isn't, is worth pinning down. It is **not** core body temperature. It's the nightly *deviation* from your own multi-night baseline, and it has no authoritative clinical reference range — the device-defined "normal" band, often around ±0.5 to ±1 °C, is a vendor-set relative cue, not a measured fact.[^jgim] Used well, it can hint at a sub-febrile shift a little before symptoms arrive. But it never stands in for a thermometer: any fever concern has to be confirmed against an actual oral reading.

## When a deviation is telling you something

A sustained move off baseline is the signal worth watching. A rising temperature is one of the clearest signs the body is mounting a response to infection — fever is a regulated host response, the body deliberately raising its own set point, not a malfunction. It rarely travels alone: a small rise tends to move with a climbing resting heart rate and a falling [heart rate variability](/learn/heart-rate-variability), and often with a higher overnight [respiratory rate](/learn/respiratory-rate). When several of those drift together, the picture is more convincing than any one of them alone.

A *low* reading deserves respect too. In an unwell person, a falling core temperature can be more sinister than a high one — it's a recognised feature of severe infection as well as of cold exposure. And older or immunocompromised people can run a serious infection with a blunted, absent, or even low temperature, so a normal number never rules infection out by itself.

<Aside type="caution">
A fever is an oral temperature **≥38.0 °C (100.4 °F)**.[^cdc] In adults the accompanying symptoms and how long they last usually matter more than the exact figure, but seek care for a fever around **≥39.4 °C (103 °F)**, or for any fever with breathlessness, chest pain, severe headache, confusion, a stiff neck, or a rash. A fever in an infant under three months always needs urgent assessment. Suspected **hypothermia** (core **&lt;35 °C / 95 °F**) or **heat stroke** (core **>40 °C / 104 °F** with confusion or collapse) is an emergency — call emergency services.[^fever] [^hypothermia]
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

Body temperature is one corner of the early-illness triad, most powerful read alongside heart rate, HRV, and breathing rather than in isolation. On its own it's a single reading, hostage to the time of day and the site you took it from. Anchored to your own baseline — same time, same site, watched as a trend — it becomes one of the most honest early signals you have.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Anchor on your own baseline; use the population ranges as a sanity check, not a scoreboard.
