Blood oxygen is the vital sign that feels most like a verdict. A number in the high nineties lands as instant reassurance — the lungs are doing their job, oxygen is reaching the blood — and a dip into the eighties reads as alarming before you've even checked whether the sensor was sitting right. Both reactions are understandable, and both can mislead. SpO₂ is genuinely useful, but it rewards a reader who knows what the percentage is actually estimating and where that estimate quietly goes wrong.

## What SpO₂ actually measures

![](/learn-img-2/blood-oxygen-spo2.jpg)

SpO₂ is the percentage of haemoglobin in your blood that is carrying oxygen, estimated without a needle by pulse oximetry — the light sensor in a fingertip clip or on the back of a wrist wearable.[^statpearls] It shines light through tissue and infers saturation from how oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood absorb it differently. The figure it produces approximates arterial saturation (SaO₂), the value a blood test would confirm, and stands in at the bedside for how well oxygen is moving from your lungs into your circulation.

That word *estimated* is the whole story. SpO₂ is not a direct reading of your blood; it is a calculation from light passing through skin, and everything between the emitter and the sensor — pigment, cold fingers, movement, nail polish, ambient light — is a place the estimate can drift.

## What's normal

For a healthy adult breathing room air at sea level, SpO₂ sits between **95 and 100%**, with most readings clustered at 96–99%.[^statpearls] Below that, the bands carry increasing weight.

| Where the reading sits | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| 95–100% (room air, sea level) | Normal for a healthy adult |
| 91–94% | Mild hypoxaemia |
| Below 90% | Clinically significant — WHO's danger floor |
| ~90% at altitude (≈2,500 m) | Can be normal; thinner air, less oxygen pressure |

Two things make this table less of a scoreboard than it looks. First, the floor moves with your circumstances: at altitude, lower ambient oxygen pressure means roughly 90% can be perfectly normal, and people with chronic lung disease such as COPD live at a lower personal baseline — applying a flat 95% alarm to them over-fires.[^statpearls] Second, there is no prize above the band. Past roughly 98%, more saturation adds nothing; "higher is always better" is the wrong goal, and in patients on supplemental oxygen, over-oxygenation is itself harmful.[^bts]

## Why wrist and finger readings vary

A medical fingertip oximeter and a consumer wrist wearable are doing the same physics with very different odds of success. The wrist is a harder place to read — the sensor sits further from a pulsing arterial bed, moves with the arm, and contends with a looser fit. Consumer optical SpO₂ is wellness-grade by design: sensitive to motion, cold or poor perfusion, nail polish and ambient light, with accuracy on the order of ±2–4%.[^statpearls]

The practical consequence is that a single low reading is more often an artefact than an emergency. Cold hands, a loose band, or a restless arm will pull the number down on a sensor that is otherwise working fine. Retake it warm, still, and well-perfused before you trust a dip. As with [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate), the sustained run under consistent conditions is the signal; the isolated reading is mostly noise.

## A known accuracy gap worth naming

There is one source of error too well-documented to leave in a footnote. Pulse oximeters can **overestimate** true oxygen saturation in people with darker skin pigmentation — the device reports a reassuring number while actual saturation is lower, a mismatch called occult hypoxaemia. A landmark 2020 analysis found this discrepancy occurred roughly three times more often in Black than in White patients, and the finding has since been reviewed by US regulators.[^sjoding][^fda]

This is not a reason to distrust the technology wholesale, but it is a reason to weight symptoms over the screen if you have darker skin. A normal-looking SpO₂ should never override breathlessness, confusion, or the plain sense that something is wrong.

<Aside type="caution">
**Pigment can make a low reading look normal.** Because pulse oximeters over-read more often on darker skin, a value in the comfortable nineties does not rule out low oxygen — trust how you feel over the number.[^sjoding][^fda] And when a low reading comes with symptoms, treat it as urgent: a sustained SpO₂ below 92% on room air, or any real breathlessness, warrants assessment, and below 90% is widely treated as a danger floor.[^statpearls] Markedly short of breath, confused, or bluish lips or skin — call emergency services. Note too that SpO₂ can stay near-normal early in carbon-monoxide poisoning, so a normal reading is no reassurance where exposure is possible.[^statpearls]
</Aside>

## When a low reading deserves attention

Most dips are the sensor, not the lungs. A few patterns are worth raising with a clinician:

- A **sustained reading below 92%** on room air, especially with any breathlessness.
- **Repeated overnight desaturations**, which are part of how sleep apnoea is screened and tie back to disrupted [sleep](/learn/sleep-consistency).
- A **falling SpO₂ alongside a rising [respiratory rate](/learn/respiratory-rate)** — the two together make a far stronger case that the lungs are under strain than either does alone.

For people with COPD or those living at altitude, the meaningful band is lower and personal; the right target is a conversation with a clinician, not a population threshold.

## The bigger picture

SpO₂ is at its most honest when it is read as a trend against your own baseline rather than a single percentage against a textbook range. A steady high-nineties line is genuine reassurance. An unexplained run of dips, particularly with breathlessness or a climbing breathing rate, is the body asking for attention. Between those two readings sit a great many cold fingers and loose wristbands — which is exactly why context, not the headline number, is the skill.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Anchor on your own baseline; use the population bands as a sanity check, not a scoreboard.
