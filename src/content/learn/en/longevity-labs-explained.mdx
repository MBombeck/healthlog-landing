A standard cholesterol panel is the bloodwork most people have done, and it quietly under-counts the thing it is meant to measure. It reports how much cholesterol is being carried, when the risk actually tracks the _number of particles_ doing the carrying — and it stays silent on an inherited risk factor you only need to measure once, and on the low-grade inflammation that turns a borderline panel into a worrying one. A handful of less familiar markers — ApoB, Lp(a), hs-CRP, and the metabolic pair of HbA1c and fasting insulin — fill in what the basic panel leaves out. None of them is a verdict. Read together, over time, they sketch a fuller picture of cardiovascular and metabolic trajectory.

## Why LDL-C is not the whole story

![](/learn-img-2/longevity-labs-explained.jpg)

The classic lipid panel measures the _mass_ of cholesterol inside your LDL particles — LDL-C. The problem is that what drives atherosclerosis is the _count_ of atherogenic particles colliding with the artery wall, and the cholesterol carried per particle varies from person to person.[^esceas] Two people can share an identical LDL-C while one carries far more, smaller, cholesterol-poor particles — and far more risk. That is the gap **ApoB** closes: one apolipoprotein-B molecule rides on each atherogenic particle, so ApoB is a direct particle count.[^esceas] **Non-HDL cholesterol** (total cholesterol minus HDL) is the cheap proxy that captures the same idea — every cholesterol fraction that isn't protective HDL — and it needs no fasting.[^esceas]

## ApoB and non-HDL: counting particles

ApoB and non-HDL are read against your overall cardiovascular risk, not a single universal "normal." The lower your background risk, the more headroom; the higher it is, the tighter the target.

| Marker    | Lower-risk orientation             | Tighter target (higher risk)                         | Note                                                     |
| --------- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| LDL-C     | under 116 mg/dL (under 3.0 mmol/L) | down to under 55 mg/dL (under 1.4) in very-high risk | Risk-category-driven, not a fixed normal[^esceas]        |
| Non-HDL-C | under 130 mg/dL (under 3.4 mmol/L) | down to under 85 mg/dL (under 2.2)                   | ≈ LDL goal + 30 mg/dL[^esceas]                           |
| ApoB      | under 100 mg/dL                    | down to under 65 mg/dL                               | Particle count; concordance check against LDL-C[^esceas] |

The targets descend in lockstep because they describe the same particles three ways. Where ApoB earns its place is _discordance_ — when ApoB sits high while LDL-C looks reassuring, the particle count is the signal to trust.[^esceas]

## Lp(a): the inherited test you do once

Lipoprotein(a) is an LDL-like particle whose blood level is roughly 80–90% genetically set and barely moves with diet, exercise or standard statins.[^lpa] A high level is an independent, causal cardiovascular risk factor — and because it is so stable, the guidance is to measure it **once in a lifetime** for most adults, to find the minority who carry an elevated inherited risk they would otherwise never know about.[^lpa] Common flags are roughly ≥50 mg/dL or ≥125 nmol/L, though the units do not interconvert cleanly and the assay matters.[^lpa] A high Lp(a) does not mean something is acutely wrong; it means your baseline risk is shifted, and it raises the priority of every other lever you _can_ move.

## hs-CRP: the inflammation layer

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein reads the low-grade inflammation that accompanies atherosclerosis. Measured for cardiovascular risk — not the same use as a generic CRP ordered for an infection — it falls into tertiles.[^hscrp]

| hs-CRP (cardiovascular use) | Band                         |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| under 1.0 mg/L              | Lower relative risk          |
| 1.0–3.0 mg/L                | Average relative risk        |
| over 3.0 mg/L               | Higher relative risk[^hscrp] |

Two cautions make or break the reading. A value above 10 mg/L is set aside entirely — it almost always means an acute infection or injury, so you repeat it once that has cleared.[^hscrp] And because any cold or flare moves it, hs-CRP is taken as the average of two measurements at least two weeks apart, well away from illness.[^hscrp] It is a risk _modifier_, layered onto the lipid picture, not a standalone number.

## The metabolic pair: HbA1c and fasting insulin

Cardiovascular and metabolic risk are the same conversation. [Blood sugar](/learn/blood-sugar-beyond-diabetes) drifts upward for years before it crosses any diabetes line, and two markers catch that drift early. **HbA1c** is the time-lapse: it reflects average glucose over the ~2–3 month lifespan of a red blood cell — normal below 5.7%, prediabetes 5.7–6.4%, diabetes at 6.5% and above.[^ada] **Fasting insulin** (and the HOMA-IR it feeds) can rise while glucose still looks fine, because the body is spending more insulin to hold the line — an early read on insulin resistance, albeit one without a single agreed cut-off.[^ada] Kidney function via **eGFR** rounds out the longevity panel, since it sits downstream of both blood pressure and glucose.[^egfr]

## Read the trend, interpret with a clinician

A single lab value sits inside a band of biological and analytical noise. The honest way to judge whether a repeat result has truly changed is the _reference change value_ — the move large enough to exceed that noise — not whether it crossed a population threshold.[^rcv] A value can stay inside the "normal" interval and still represent a real personal shift, and the reverse. So the same discipline that governs a [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) baseline applies to bloodwork: watch the direction across repeated tests, taken under the same conditions.

<Aside type="caution">
  These markers interact, and the guideline bands themselves disagree at the
  edges — ESC/EAS publishes explicit numeric LDL and ApoB goals, while other
  bodies prefer percent-reduction targets. A lab panel is interpreted as a
  whole, against your personal risk, by a clinician who can see your history and
  medications — not decoded from a screenshot. Treat a single out-of-range value
  as a reason to ask a question, never as a diagnosis.
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

The basic lipid panel is not wrong, just incomplete. ApoB and non-HDL count the particles that actually drive risk; Lp(a) names an inherited risk you measure once and then plan around; hs-CRP adds the inflammation layer; HbA1c and fasting insulin catch metabolic drift years before a diagnosis. Set beside your [blood pressure](/learn/reading-your-blood-pressure), your [cardiorespiratory fitness](/learn/vo2max-and-longevity) and your [waist](/learn/waist-and-central-fat), these labs turn a once-a-year cholesterol number into a trajectory you can read — and, with a clinician, change.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Reference intervals are assay- and lab-dependent; interpret any result with the clinician who ordered it, against your own history, not against a single threshold.
