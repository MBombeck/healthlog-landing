Glucose has a branding problem. For most people it lives in one mental folder marked *diabetes* — a number their doctor checks if something has gone wrong, irrelevant otherwise. But the glucose circulating in your blood right now is simply your body's primary fuel, and the amount of it reflects a moment-to-moment balance struck by what you ate, how you moved, how you slept, and the hormones managing all three. Read well, it is one of the more honest mirrors of metabolic health you have — long before any threshold is crossed.

## What blood glucose actually is

![](/learn-img-2/blood-sugar-beyond-diabetes.jpg)

Glucose is the sugar your cells burn for energy, carried in the bloodstream and topped up by the carbohydrate in your food. After a meal it rises; insulin then ushers it into cells and the level falls back toward baseline. In a metabolically healthy person that whole arc is quiet and quick — a modest rise, a smooth return. The interesting information is in the shape of that curve, not just its peak.

Health trackers tend to capture glucose in three different ways, because each answers a different question:

- **Fasting plasma glucose (FPG)** — a single reading after at least eight hours without caloric intake. It shows your baseline with no recent meal in play.[^who2006]
- **Postprandial glucose** — a reading after eating, classically two hours into a standardised 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). It shows how quickly your system clears a carbohydrate load.[^ada2025]
- **HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin)** — the fraction of your haemoglobin that has glucose bound to it, integrated over the roughly two-to-three-month lifespan of a red blood cell.[^ada2025] It is the long-run average, not a snapshot.

A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) adds a fourth view: thousands of interstitial readings a day, summarised as *time in range* rather than any single value.

## The spot reading versus the running average

This is the distinction worth holding onto. A fasting or post-meal reading is a photograph — true for the instant the needle or sensor sampled, and easily nudged by last night's dinner, this morning's stress, or a cold coming on. HbA1c is a time-lapse. Because glucose binds to haemoglobin irreversibly and red cells live for months, HbA1c can't be gamed by a single good or bad day; it reports the company your blood sugar has kept over the preceding weeks.[^ada2025]

Neither one is "the real number." A normal HbA1c with repeatedly high fasting spikes tells a different story than a creeping HbA1c with unremarkable spot readings. They are most useful side by side — the photograph explains the day, the time-lapse explains the trend.

<Aside type="note" title="Plasma versus fingerstick">
Diagnostic thresholds are defined on venous plasma drawn in a lab. A capillary fingerstick from a glucometer reads slightly below venous plasma, so treat the number on a home device as oriented to — not identical to — the cut-points below.[^who2006]
</Aside>

## What the ranges say

All the figures below are general screening and management references, not a diagnosis. A single abnormal reading never diagnoses anything on its own: the American Diabetes Association requires a repeat abnormal test on a separate day before confirming.[^ada2025]

**Fasting plasma glucose**

| Category | mg/dL · mmol/L | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal (ADA) | under 100 · under 5.6 | ADA, 2025[^ada2025] |
| Prediabetes / IFG (ADA) | 100–125 · 5.6–6.9 | ADA, 2025[^ada2025] |
| Prediabetes / IFG (WHO) | 110–125 · 6.1–6.9 | WHO/IDF, 2006[^who2006] |
| Diabetes (confirmed) | 126 or higher · 7.0 or higher | ADA 2025; WHO/IDF 2006[^ada2025] |

The lower edge of the prediabetes band is where the two bodies disagree — the ADA opens it at 100 mg/dL, the WHO at 110.[^who2006] The diabetes threshold of 126 mg/dL is shared.

**Postprandial / 2-hour OGTT** — the ADA and WHO/IDF use identical cut-points here.[^ada2025]

| Category | mg/dL · mmol/L | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | under 140 · under 7.8 | ADA 2025; WHO/IDF 2006[^ada2025] |
| Impaired glucose tolerance | 140–199 · 7.8–11.0 | ADA 2025; WHO/IDF 2006[^ada2025] |
| Diabetes | 200 or higher · 11.1 or higher | ADA 2025; WHO/IDF 2006[^ada2025] |

A random plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher with classic symptoms is independently diagnostic.[^ada2025]

**HbA1c**

| Category | % · mmol/mol | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | under 5.7 · under 39 | ADA, 2025[^ada2025] |
| Prediabetes | 5.7–6.4 · 39–46 | ADA, 2025[^ada2025] |
| Diabetes (confirmed) | 6.5 or higher · 48 or higher | ADA 2025; WHO 2011[^who2011] |

The WHO endorses the 6.5% diabetes cut-off but does not define a formal prediabetes HbA1c band — a difference of scope, not a contradiction.[^who2011] As orientation, an HbA1c near 7% corresponds roughly to a CGM time-in-range near 70%, where "in range" means 70–180 mg/dL.[^battelino] That time-in-range target, and the rest of the CGM dashboard, assume active diabetes management — they are not a non-diabetic optimisation goal.[^battelino]

To convert units: glucose **mg/dL ÷ 18.0 = mmol/L**; HbA1c **mmol/mol = (% − 2.15) × 10.929**.

## What drives the day-to-day variation

Glucose responds to far more than your last plate of food. Acute illness, physical or emotional stress, corticosteroid medication, and a recent carbohydrate load all push readings up — sometimes sharply, almost always temporarily. Sleep and timing matter too: even one night of partial sleep deprivation measurably lowers insulin sensitivity, and glucose tolerance falls across the day, so an identical meal produces a higher spike in the evening than at breakfast.[^cross] A poor night or a late, heavy dinner is a perfectly plausible reason for an isolated next-morning fasting anomaly — and not, by itself, evidence of anything.

This is exactly why the running average is so steadying. One alarming spot reading taken on a bad day says little; a fasting number that has quietly drifted into the prediabetes band across many mornings, or an HbA1c trending upward over successive tests, is the signal worth acting on. A meaningful change in serial HbA1c is roughly half a percentage point or more — smaller wobbles sit inside normal assay and biological variation.[^cross]

<Aside type="caution" title="The floors that need attention — and the ceiling that can mislead">
These thresholds describe glucose itself; none of them is a self-diagnosis. A glucose **under 70 mg/dL (under 3.9 mmol/L)** is a hypoglycaemia alert regardless of symptoms — treat with fast-acting carbohydrate and re-check in 15 minutes. **Under 54 mg/dL (under 3.0 mmol/L)** is clinically significant and needs immediate action.[^redflags] In the other direction, sustained very high glucose (commonly 250–300 mg/dL or above) with thirst, frequent urination, vomiting, fast breathing or altered thinking warrants prompt care. Do not be falsely reassured by one figure: diabetic ketoacidosis can occur even when glucose is only mildly raised — sometimes around 200 mg/dL, particularly with certain medications — so if you feel unwell a "normal-looking" reading is not an all-clear.[^redflags] Separately, HbA1c becomes unreliable wherever red-cell turnover or haemoglobin is altered — anaemia, recent transfusion, pregnancy, haemoglobinopathies, advanced kidney disease — and direct glucose readings take precedence there.[^ada2025]
</Aside>

## Why this matters for everyone

The reason to watch glucose before any threshold is crossed is simple: the window where ordinary lifestyle change still prevents progression opens long before symptoms do.[^ada2025] Chronic elevation quietly damages small vessels — eyes, kidneys, nerves — and larger ones, and most of that road is travelled without a single noticeable warning. Catching an upward drift while the numbers are still merely "creeping" is the entire case for tracking early.

## The bigger picture

Glucose never travels alone. Weight is the most actionable lever most people have — a sustained 5–10% loss meaningfully improves glycaemia alongside blood pressure and lipids, and central fat in particular tracks with insulin resistance, which is why it pairs naturally with [what the scale doesn't tell you](/learn/beyond-the-scale). [Sleep consistency](/learn/sleep-consistency) feeds straight back into your morning fasting number, and the same metabolic and autonomic machinery surfaces in [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) and [heart rate variability](/learn/heart-rate-variability). HbA1c and fasting insulin also sit at the heart of the [longevity labs](/learn/longevity-labs-explained) that catch metabolic drift years early. Read together, these signals describe a system; read alone, each is only a fragment of it.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual variation is wide. Anchor on your own baseline; use the population ranges as a sanity check, not a scoreboard.
