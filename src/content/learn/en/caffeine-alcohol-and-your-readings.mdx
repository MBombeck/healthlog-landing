Two of the most common things people put into their bodies are also two of the most visible influences on the numbers a health tracker collects. Caffeine and alcohol both act on the same autonomic nervous system that governs heart rate, blood pressure, and the quiet machinery of sleep — and they do it in ways that show up clearly the next morning. Once you know what to look for, a surprising share of "bad" readings stop being mysteries and start being receipts.

## Caffeine: a stimulant with a long tail

![](/learn-img-2/caffeine-alcohol-and-your-readings.jpg)

Caffeine works by blocking adenosine, the molecule that builds up through the day and makes you feel sleepy. With the brake released, the sympathetic "accelerator" runs a little freer: heart rate ticks up, and blood pressure rises transiently — an acute effect that is most pronounced in people who don't drink it regularly, and that blunts with habitual use.[^vlachopoulos] None of this is dangerous in ordinary amounts, but it does mean a coffee an hour before you sit down to measure can nudge both your [resting heart rate](/learn/resting-heart-rate) and your blood pressure upward enough to notice.

The part that catches people out is timing. Caffeine's half-life in a healthy adult is commonly cited at around five hours, meaning an afternoon cup still has half its dose circulating at bedtime.[^institute] In a controlled study, a dose equivalent to a strong coffee taken **six hours before bed** measurably reduced total sleep time — participants underestimated the disruption, because the effect on sleep was larger than the effect they could feel.[^drake] That gap between felt and measured is exactly why a tracker is useful here.

| When you have it | What tends to show up |
| --- | --- |
| Within an hour of measuring | Transiently higher pulse and blood pressure |
| Mid-to-late afternoon | Quietly fragmented or shortened sleep that night |
| Consistently, every day | Smaller acute spikes as tolerance builds |

## Alcohol: the deceptive nightcap

Alcohol is the more interesting case, because it does the opposite of what it feels like it's doing. A drink is sedating, so it can shorten the time it takes to fall asleep — but as the body metabolises it through the night, sleep architecture pays the bill. Reviews of the evidence find that alcohol suppresses REM sleep early in the night and triggers a rebound and more fragmented, lighter sleep in the second half, even at moderate doses.[^ebrahim] You spend longer in bed and less of it actually restored.

The autonomic cost is just as visible. As the body works to clear alcohol, the sympathetic side stays switched on while you sleep: resting heart rate runs **elevated overnight**, and [heart rate variability](/learn/heart-rate-variability) — the beat-to-beat flexibility that signals a relaxed, recovering body — drops. A study tracking these signals across normal nights found that an evening's drinking measurably raised nocturnal heart rate and lowered HRV, with larger amounts producing larger and longer-lasting effects.[^pietila] This is why a single glass with dinner can quietly flatten your recovery score while you sleep through the whole thing.

Alcohol also moves [blood sugar](/learn/blood-sugar-beyond-diabetes), though less predictably. The liver prioritises clearing alcohol over its usual job of releasing glucose, which can push readings down — sometimes hours later, overnight — an interaction that matters most for anyone on glucose-lowering medication.[^diabetesuk]

## Why this explains so many "bad" mornings

Put the two together and a familiar pattern emerges. A glass of wine in the evening, an espresso that came a little later in the day than you remember, and the next morning your resting heart rate is up several beats, your HRV is down, and your sleep tracker is unimpressed — despite a full night in bed. Nothing has gone wrong with your body. You're seeing the overnight cost of two substances behaving exactly as their pharmacology predicts.

This is the practical value of watching your own data over time. Rather than treating each substance as forbidden or harmless in the abstract, you can see *your* response: how late is too late for caffeine before your sleep frays, how much alcohol it takes before your overnight heart rate climbs, and how long the recovery takes. The answers are personal, and the [stress and recovery](/learn/stress-and-recovery) signals make them legible.

<Aside type="note">
This isn't an argument for abstinence. It's an argument for attribution. If you understand that last night's drink is why this morning's numbers look rough, you stop mistaking a predictable, temporary dip for a sign that something is wrong — and you stop chasing fixes for a problem that resolves itself by tomorrow.
</Aside>

## A few honest caveats

Individual variation here is wide. Caffeine metabolism is partly genetic — some people clear it quickly and sleep fine after an evening coffee, while slow metabolisers feel a single afternoon cup well into the night.[^institute] Alcohol's effects scale with dose, timing relative to sleep, and body size, and they compound with poor sleep and stress rather than acting in isolation.

A wrist-worn optical sensor reads overnight heart rate and HRV well at rest, but it can only show you *that* recovery dipped, not catalogue every cause. Treat the alcohol-and-HRV link as one of the most reliable patterns in consumer health data — it shows up again and again — while remembering that a single rough night can have many overlapping explanations, of which last night's drink is only the most common.

<Aside type="caution">
If you take medication that lowers blood pressure or blood glucose, alcohol can amplify those effects in ways a tracker won't fully capture — including delayed overnight lows. The interactions are worth raising with a clinician or pharmacist rather than reasoning out from your readings alone.
</Aside>

## The bigger picture

Caffeine and alcohol are not problems to be solved; they're variables to be understood. Both leave fingerprints on the same handful of metrics — resting heart rate, HRV, sleep, and to a lesser extent glucose — and once you can read those fingerprints, your morning numbers become far less mysterious and far more useful. The goal isn't to eliminate the inputs. It's to stop being surprised by the outputs.

This is general information, not medical advice, and individual responses vary widely. Anchor on your own baseline, watch how your readings move with your habits, and use that pattern — not a rule from an article — to decide what works for you.
